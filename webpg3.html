Phishing & Social Engineering

Most attackers do not hack your device. They trick you. This guide explains how phishing works, how to spot it, and how to stay safe.

1. What is phishing?

Phishing is a type of social engineering attack. The attacker pretends to be someone you trust, such as a bank, a delivery company, your school or your boss. The goal is to make you share private information, click a harmful link or open a dangerous file.

Why do attackers rely on human psychology?

  • It is cheap: one message can be sent to millions of people.
  • People are easier to fool than computers: a convincing story can get past good security.
  • Emotions override logic: fear, curiosity and urgency make people click before thinking.
  • It pays: stolen passwords and card details can be used or sold.

2. Types of phishing

Phishing arrives through many channels. There are five main types.

Email Phishing

Mass emails that copy well-known brands and ask you to click a link or log in.

Example: "Your streaming account has been suspended."

Spear Phishing

A targeted attack that uses your name, job or interests so it looks personal.

Example: A fake email from your "manager" asking for a quick favour.

Smishing

Phishing sent by SMS or messaging apps, usually with a short link.

Example: "Parcel fee unpaid. Pay now or it will be returned."

Vishing

Voice phishing: phone calls from fake "bank" or "tech support" staff.

Example: "We detected fraud. Please confirm your PIN."

Clone Phishing

A real email you already received is copied and resent with a harmful link or file.

Example: "Resending the invoice. Updated version attached."

3. How a phishing attack usually progresses

  1. Research: the attacker picks a target and collects details from social media or data leaks.
  2. Bait: a believable message is sent using a trusted name and logo.
  3. Hook: the message creates pressure (a deadline, a threat or a reward) so you act fast.
  4. Capture: a fake login page steals your password, or a file installs malware.
  5. Exploit: your account is taken over, money is stolen, or your contacts get the same attack.

4. Warning signs

Stop and think if a message has any of these red flags.

⚠ Artificial urgency

"Act within 24 hours or lose access!" Pressure is meant to stop you thinking.

⚠ Mismatched sender domain

The name looks right, but the real email address uses a strange or misspelled domain.

⚠ Deceptive URLs

Hover over a link before clicking. If the address does not match the company, do not click.

⚠ Credential requests

Real companies never ask for your password, PIN or one-time code by email, SMS or phone.

Also watch for: generic greetings like "Dear Customer", spelling mistakes, and unexpected attachments such as .zip or .exe files.

5. How to protect yourself

✔ Verify independently

Do not use the contact details in the message. Open the official app, type the website address yourself, or call the number on your bank card.

✔ Inspect URLs

Check the domain name (the part just before ".com"). Look for misspellings and extra words like "secure-login".

✔ Use multi-factor authentication (MFA)

Even if your password is stolen, a second step such as a phone code can stop the attacker.

✔ Avoid unexpected attachments

Do not open files you were not expecting, even from people you know. Ask them through another channel first.

If you already clicked

  1. Disconnect from the internet if you opened a file.
  2. Change the password on that account (and anywhere you reused it).
  3. Turn on multi-factor authentication.
  4. Report the message to your school, workplace or the company being copied.

6. Annotated example message

This is a fictional phishing email. The numbers [1] to [5] mark the red flags, which are explained underneath.

From: "AcmeBank Security" <alert@acmebank-secure-login.example> [1]

Subject: URGENT: Your account will be closed in 24 hours! [2]

Dear Customer, [3]

We found unusual activity on your account. To avoid permanent closure, verify your identity now:

http://acmebank.verify-login-now.example/secure [4]

If the link does not work, reply with your password and PIN. [5]

Thank you,
AcmeBank Security Team

Red flags explained

  1. [1] Mismatched sender domain: "acmebank-secure-login.example" is not the bank's real website.
  2. [2] Artificial urgency: a 24-hour threat to pressure you.
  3. [3] Generic greeting: a real bank would use your name.
  4. [4] Deceptive URL: the link does not match the bank and does not use HTTPS.
  5. [5] Credential request: no real bank asks for your password or PIN by email.
